WSJ – Best of the Web: "The Times That Tried Men’s Souls"

2:38pm PT

  • The Times That Tried Men’s Souls. Even though Thanksgiving has passed, the newsletter says Americans have reason to keep being grateful by reflecting on events of 250 years ago—times when the founding generation blundered but refused to quit.
  • In late autumn 1776, months after the Declaration, Washington and what was left of his army were on the run through New Jersey after the British kicked them out of New York. A June review of Jack Kelly’s Tom Paine’s War by Jeffrey Collins noted the failures that led there: on Nov. 16, 1776, Fort Washington fell, nearly 3,000 patriots were taken prisoner, and 2,000 of them later died in captivity. Washington, watching from Fort Lee, is said to have wept.
  • The American position was perilous: New York was lost, British troops advancing from Canada threatened to bisect the colonies, Britain commanded the seas, and seasoned troops plus Hessian mercenaries faced a poorly provisioned colonial army, with no shortage of Loyalists in New York and New Jersey. After Fort Washington, the Continental Army made a panicked retreat from Fort Lee south through New Jersey, across the Delaware into Pennsylvania—some without shoes, most wrapped in blankets. Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene called it “a very pitiful army to trust the Liberties of America upon.”
  • On this day in 1776, Washington was still in New Jersey, just beginning the retreat to Pennsylvania and urging Gen. Charles Lee to bring his troops. Mount Vernon describes the day.

The Spectator – Evening Blend: "The Badenoch paradox: hate the Lib Dems, love their voters"

10:25am PT, by Tim Shipman

  • Three moments united the room at the Spectator’s Parliamentarian of the Year awards on Wednesday: Michael Gove roasting Andrew Griffith and Robert Jenrick (to the delight of Nigel Farage and Zia Yusuf); Farage winning universal acclaim for accepting the main award on behalf of Ann Widdecombe and saying British politicians can have fun together even when they disagree; and a host joke about Zack Polanski. Nothing brought the room together like David Lammy noting he hadn’t mentioned the Lib Dems and planned to keep it that way.
  • The Lib Dems still get under the skins of other parties—Reform finds them a nuisance in council by-elections, and Tories bemoan them campaigning as nicer Conservatives in some places and more radical than Labour in others—but Tory campaigners now see wealthy southern Lib Dem voters, who dislike both Reform and Andy Burnham’s Labour, as their top targets. Reform officials were delighted to find an internal Tory document citing Lynton Crosby’s “go duck hunting where the ducks are”: the focus is identifying and repeatedly persuading soft voters. Shipman notes every senior Tory at conference already said the official strategy is to talk tough on the economy to win back Lib Dems, overtake Reform in the polls, then peel off the third of Reform supporters who just want to back the leading party of the right.
  • The problem for Kemi Badenoch, as he argued in his magazine column this week, is that Brexit’s return to the forefront divides sovereignty-first Tories from economy-first centrists and forces her to woo a group who were predominantly Remain voters in 2016 (as were a third of existing Tory voters). To counter the charge that she has surrounded herself with Remainers, she announced that all candidates will sign a covenant ahead of the next election committing them to leaving the ECHR, staying out of the EU, and scrapping net-zero targets—a level of pre-election control never asserted before, even compared with Boris Johnson kicking out 21 MPs who would not back his approach.

The Spectator – Americano: "A tribute to the Trump Thanksgiving Truth Tirade"

8:01am PT, by Freddy Gray

  • Freddy Gray unpacks President Trump’s Thanksgiving Truth Social post.
  • Trump wished a happy Thanksgiving to “American Patriots” and, sarcastically, to those who issued “fake Economic Numbers” just before the election—revised yesterday, three weeks late, from 3.4% to 4.3% GDP—which he called a “RECORD SETTING ERROR” that should be investigated. Gray notes the revision was only the most significant since 2020, not a record, and that Trump later reposted Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on the same subject, even though the BEA sits in Lutnick’s own department. Gray asks how Lutnick’s team, including Under Secretary for Economic Affairs Joyce Y. Meyer, let a supposedly nefarious underwhelming estimate go through.
  • Trump also wished a happy Thanksgiving to those he said have worked to bring the country to the “Radical Far Left,” change the court system to Sharia, or redistribute property. Gray reads this as Trump “kidding on the square,” hoping that Democrats—now that Republicans have lost their congressional majority—will remind the public in 2027 how radical they can be, while Trump offers to work across the aisle on energy and healthcare.
  • In a Minnesota section, Trump thanked “the 2% of our Somalian population that have a job” and said they should be allowed to stay, while “the others should be thrown out, immediately.” Gray calls this classic Trump sarcasm, noting Minnesota has been the scene of many anti-ICE protests in his second term.

WSJ – Books: "Ringing in the telephone era; the rise of Charlemagne; Dustin Hoffman"

7:16am PT, by WSJ Books Staff

  • The Telephone: Charles C. Mann reviews James Gleick’s book on the trial-and-error experiments through which Alexander Graham Bell and others sought to make remote conversation a reality, and how far the predictions of early enthusiasts—and critics—were justified. In 1877, Bell predicted telephone service would one day be supplied to homes “just as gas and water are supplied—not luxuries but necessities.” That same year, an Illinois newspaper editor warned of the “frightful possibilities” of constant stimulation and intrusive news. As Mann writes, widespread adoption offered connection to the formerly isolated and even changed the human perception of time.
  • Also: Jonathan W. Jordan on Washington’s sharpshooters; Benjamin Shull on travel in the ancient world; Meghan Cox Gurdon on the history of the pen.
  • Frank Ambition: Patrick J. Geary on “Charlemagne’s Dream: The Rise and Fall of the Carolingian Empire” by James T. Palmer. The idea of Europe as a distinct cultural entity has its roots in the achievements of the eighth-century Frankish conqueror Charles Martel—whose military and political triumphs gave him control over what had been Roman Gaul—and the further successes of his grandson, Charlemagne. By the year 800, when Charlemagne was crowned emperor by Pope Leo III, the Carolingian dynasty had brought relative peace and stability to a large portion of the continent.
  • Fiction: Sam Sacks on “Fabrications” by Zachary Mason, a collection of stories exploring the possibilities of living with artificial-intelligence companions.
  • Nonfiction: The American Table: Memories of a Good Meal by Alon Shaya with June Hersh. Dreams of future dishes gave sustenance to soldiers and civilians who had to endure the deprivations of World War II.
