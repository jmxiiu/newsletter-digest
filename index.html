Tech and AI

WSJ AI & Business: Anthropic's Big Biotech Promise

9:06am PT, by David Wainer. AI Thinks It Can Out-Code Cancer. Human Biology Says Not So Fast

  • In Silicon Valley, Dario Amodei is known as the AI boss who worries too much. In biotech, he's the one who worries too little. Ahead of Anthropic's initial public offering this week, Amodei doubled down on his view that AI could make diseases such as cancer and Alzheimer's relics of the past within a decade. It is a breathtaking promise, and at least a part of the story behind Anthropic's $1.3 trillion valuation. The company isn't just selling a better chatbot or a faster coding assistant. It's selling the prospect of transforming enormous industries.
  • Yet the people who make drugs aren't so sure. "I think that would be amazing and I think it's not likely," Eli Lilly CEO Dave Ricks wrote on X. "The map of biology is still largely a blank page and randomized human proof is the long pole in the tent."
  • There is little doubt AI can speed up parts of drug development. It can help scientists scour research, design molecules and analyze experiments. But discovering a promising molecule is a long way from proving it is safe and effective in people. That requires clinical trials. Those can take years and can't simply be accelerated with more computing power. The bigger problem is that AI can't uncover what biology hasn't yet revealed. And, for most diseases, the data simply doesn't exist.
  • Daphne Koller, a Stanford computer scientist and founder of AI drug-discovery company insitro, is a believer in the technology. But in an August essay, she argued that because scientists understand so little about the biology of most diseases, AI "will mostly help us generate failures faster." Even with the right drug in hand, the clock is hard to beat. An Alzheimer's trial has to wait years to see whether patients' cognitive decline has slowed, and there are only so many patients and trial sites to go around. "A lot of tech people are accustomed to seeing exponential growth, where things are just infinitely compressible because you're dealing in digital goods," Koller said in an interview. "The real world compresses only so far."
  • Anthropic's own drug ambitions illustrate the challenge. The company recently acquired Mithril Medicine, a biotech with a single employee. Its founder used Claude to analyze data, plan experiments, and coordinate outside labs to pursue a treatment for a rare form of childhood muscular dystrophy. Anthropic's bet is that this could represent a new model that could be replicated on multiple projects at once. Even if early results hold up, the program won't enter human trials until 2028. Roughly nine in 10 drugs that enter clinical trials fail to win approval. If it does succeed, it could take years more to reach patients.
  • Anthropic's IPO filing touts AI's potential to compress decades of scientific progress into years, while warning that drug candidates face "lengthy, costly, and uncertain" development and may never generate revenue. Investors should pay attention to the disconnect between technological possibility and commercial reality.
  • A Campaign Is Afoot To Bring Back Jensen Huang: Never say never. But it seems pretty far-fetched. A huge contingent of Huang's allies and cheerleaders in Silicon Valley are campaigning for his return after he resigned as the company's chief executive last week. President Trump weighed in on Monday, saying he wouldn't mind if it happened. From a legal and political perspective, though, it would be risky. Huang violated company policy by having relationships with subordinates and a customer who U.S. intelligence believes was spying for China. That is a lot for a company to try to move past, even if more allegations against Huang don't emerge. Unsurprisingly, the company's board isn't considering the idea—at least for now.
  • The Number: $50 Billion — Minimum amount UBS analyst Tim Arcuri estimates could be taken out of the AI ecosystem if Nvidia dials back its financing activities with Jensen Huang's departure.
  • What the Humans Are Saying: "I reject the notion that we can pass off the blame to machines. The people who create and operate these machines need to accept the responsibility for their actions." — Rob Bonta, California Attorney General, after suing OpenAI over agent-driven attacks.
  • AI in Charts: Nvidia's longtime chief executive may be gone, but the chip giant is still a cash-generating machine. The company generated $127 billion of free cash flow in its most recent four reported quarters. Analysts expect it to bring in more than $261 billion in the ensuing year. Nvidia already has a large $230 billion share buyback authorization through the end of its 2028 fiscal year. If the company's new leadership backs off on aggressive financial commitments aimed at juicing chip demand, which seems likely, it would free up more cash to put back in investors' hands.
  • AI in the Wild: Data centers are facing a lot of opposition on land, which is one reason Elon Musk thinks putting them in space makes sense. But what about in the ocean? Several companies are starting to explore the idea of data centers in the water. Some would be untethered from land, generating electricity through solar panels and the motion of waves while sending data via satellite. Other ideas involve boats moored at ports.
  • Other Highlights From the Week in AI: Nvidia may not be the bank of AI for much longer. The Trump administration plans to designate Nvidia a "national champion" despite all the turmoil at the company. OpenAI debuted a wallet that allows users' agents to pay for things.
